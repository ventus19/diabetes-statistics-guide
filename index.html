Prologue

0. Introduction: Statistical Analysis Translates Hypotheses into Data Structures

When studying the relationship between lifestyle factors and glycemic control in patients with diabetes, statistical analysis is not merely a procedure for producing p-values. It is a stepwise process for examining how complex lifestyle factors such as exercise, diet, sleep, stress, and body mass index are associated with glycated hemoglobin and fasting blood glucose, and whether those associations remain after controlling for other clinical factors.

Core Principle
Descriptive statistics characterize the sample, group-difference tests compare means, correlation analysis describes linear associations, and regression analysis estimates conditional associations while controlling multiple variables simultaneously.
Caution About the Example Data
The N=150, means, correlation coefficients, and regression coefficients in this document are example values supplied for manuscript-writing practice. They must not be interpreted as results from actual patient data or used for clinical decision-making.
Chapter 1

1. Research Model and Hypothesis Development

A hypothesis should be written as a testable statement that includes directionality and measurable variables. Rather than saying “exercise is good for blood glucose,” it is more appropriate to specify the expected quantitative relationship between exercise volume and HbA1c.

H1 ExerciseLonger weekly aerobic exercise duration will be associated with lower HbA1c.
H2 Dietary HabitsMore frequent intake of simple sugars and high-sodium foods will be associated with higher fasting blood glucose (FBS).
H3 Self-ManagementThe frequency of self-monitoring of blood glucose will be positively associated with glycemic-control achievement.

Limits on Causal Language
In a cross-sectional study, it is safer to write “was associated with” or “showed a higher/lower tendency” rather than “caused” or “affected.” Causal inference requires temporal ordering and adequate control of confounding.

The difference in HbA1c by sex was not statistically significant (t=1.124, p=.263), whereas the exercise group had a lower mean HbA1c than the non-exercise group (t=-4.312, p<.001).

Checks Before Testing

  • Are the two groups independent?
  • What are the distributions and extreme values in each group?
  • If homogeneity of variance is violated, should Welch’s t-test be used?
  • Are Cohen’s d or Hedges’ g reported along with the p-value?

If the omnibus F-test is significant, conduct post hoc tests to identify which groups differ. When homogeneity of variance is met, Scheffé or Tukey tests may be used; when it is violated, consider Welch ANOVA with Games-Howell comparisons.

Limitations of Categorizing Age
Arbitrarily categorizing continuous age can lead to information loss and reduced statistical power. Categorize age only when age-group comparisons are substantively needed, and preferentially consider using age as a continuous variable in regression analysis.

Effect Size

Report η² or ω² with ANOVA results to indicate the magnitude of group differences. A p-value addresses evidence of a difference but does not directly indicate clinical importance.

Interpretation Principles

  • A negative correlation between exercise duration and HbA1c indicates that longer exercise duration tends to be associated with lower HbA1c.
  • A positive correlation between BMI and HbA1c indicates that higher BMI tends to be associated with higher HbA1c.
  • The absolute value of the correlation coefficient reflects the strength of the association, while statistical significance should be assessed using sample size and the p-value or confidence interval.
  • A correlation smaller than r=-.30 is not automatically a “significant correlation.”
Inappropriate Wording

“Exercise has been proven to reduce HbA1c.”

Recommended Wording

“Weekly exercise duration showed a moderate negative correlation with HbA1c (r=-.384).”

Interpreting Coefficients

  • A B coefficient of .084 for BMI means that, after controlling for other variables, a one-unit increase in BMI is associated with a 0.084 percentage-point increase in HbA1c.
  • If exercise duration is measured in hours, B=-.125 means that each additional hour of weekly exercise is associated with a 0.125 percentage-point decrease in HbA1c.
  • Standardized coefficients (β) can be used to compare the relative magnitude of associations across predictors measured in different units, but they should not be interpreted as a causal ranking of importance.
  • R²=.452 means that the model explains 45.2% of the variation in HbA1c within the sample; it does not guarantee predictive performance in external samples.

VIF and Durbin-Watson
VIF<10 is a widely known but lenient rule of thumb, whereas VIF<5 can be used as a more conservative threshold. A Durbin-Watson statistic near 2 suggests limited residual autocorrelation, but cross-sectional data still require consideration of the sampling structure and clustering.

Additional Diagnostics

  • Check linearity and homoscedasticity with residual-versus-fitted plots
  • Check the residual distribution with a Q-Q plot
  • Assess influential observations using Cook’s distance and leverage
  • If observations are clustered by institution, ward, or region, consider multilevel models or cluster-robust standard errors

10. Writing Results and Discussion

t-Test

The mean HbA1c did not differ significantly by sex (t=1.124, p=.263). In contrast, the exercise group had a lower mean HbA1c than the non-exercise group, and the between-group difference was statistically significant (t=-4.312, p<.001).

ANOVA

Mean HbA1c differed significantly across age groups (F=5.842, p=.004). Scheffé post hoc testing showed that the group aged 60 years or older had higher HbA1c than the groups in their 40s and 50s.

Correlation Analysis

Weekly exercise duration was negatively correlated with HbA1c (r=-.384, p<.001), whereas BMI was positively correlated with HbA1c (r=.412, p<.001).

Multiple Regression

The multiple regression model was statistically significant (F=29.845, p<.001) and explained 45.2% of the variance in HbA1c (R²=.452, adjusted R²=.436). BMI was positively associated with HbA1c, whereas exercise duration and diet management score were negatively associated with HbA1c.
Good Results Reporting
Report test statistics, degrees of freedom, p-values, effect sizes, confidence intervals, and the analysis N, and distinguish statistical significance from clinical importance in the discussion.

11. Advanced Study Design and Limitations of Interpretation

Confounding ControlConsider medication type, disease duration, complications, and socioeconomic status
MediationExamine whether exercise is associated with HbA1c through BMI or stress
InteractionExamine whether the association of exercise differs by age, sex, or treatment type
Longitudinal DesignAssess the temporal relationship between changes in lifestyle and subsequent changes in HbA1c

Common Study Limitations

  • Recall and social-desirability bias in self-reported lifestyle measures
  • Potential reverse causation in cross-sectional data
  • Limited generalizability from a single-institution sample
  • Mismatch between clinical measurements and survey data collected at different times
  • Overfitting and multiple-testing concerns during model selection

Epilogue

15. Conclusion: From Statistical Significance to Clinical Interpretation

A strong medical statistics paper is not one that merely produces tables with small p-values; it is one that selects analyses appropriate to the research hypotheses and data structure and transparently explains the magnitude, uncertainty, and limitations of the findings.

Descriptive statistics, difference tests, correlation analysis, and regression analysis are not interchangeable methods; each addresses a different question at a different stage. When explaining the relationship between lifestyle and glycemic control, effect sizes, confidence intervals, confounders, measurement error, and temporal relationships should all be considered.

Final Practical Workflow
Develop hypotheses → Define variables → Assess data quality → Descriptive statistics → Difference/correlation analyses → Multivariable adjustment → Assumption diagnostics → Report effect sizes/confidence intervals → Interpret clinical meaning and limitations
